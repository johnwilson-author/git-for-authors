The coast road is not old. That is the first thing to understand about it, and
the thing that most people who drive it assume is wrong.

The road as it exists — two lanes, a wall on the seaward side, four bridges —
was built between 1821 and 1826, by men whose names are recorded in a ledger
that survives in the county archive and has been read by almost nobody. What
was there before was not a road in any sense a driver would recognise. It was
a set of paths, some of them Roman, most of them older, none of them
continuous.

The distinction matters because the road's history is usually told as a story
of improvement: a bad path became a good road. The archive does not support
that. It supports something more interesting — that the road was built to move
things that had previously been moved by sea, and that the villages along it
resisted it, and that one of them resisted it successfully for four years.
